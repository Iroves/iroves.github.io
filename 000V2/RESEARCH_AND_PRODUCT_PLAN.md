# Emergency Medical ID: evidence-informed product research and design plan

Research date: 9 October 2026. Primary jurisdiction: Australia. Prepared for concept exploration; not individual medical, legal, or regulatory advice.

## Executive concept

A card or wearable carrying NFC + printed QR points to a short, highly readable emergency record that a bystander or responder may consult after calling emergency help. Prioritise the first ~10 seconds: identify the person when appropriate; identify severe allergies/major risks; tap primary/backup contact. A clinician may benefit from more detail further down. A digital scan should **not delay a 000 call or urgent first aid**. NFC and QR both lead to URLs; neither guarantees that emergency services will scan them or that mobile data will work. Complement with a medical alert bracelet and wallet paper card as needed.

## Helper-first order in version 2

This product belongs to the individual who might have been injured, become disoriented or become unable to speak. The primary screen is intended for a bystander, family member, or responder assisting that person—not as a personal health dashboard. The first sequence is deliberately simple: **(1) Call 000 if there is a medical emergency, (2) call the nominated main emergency contact (e.g. spouse/husband), (3) review severe allergies and major medical risks, (4) review current medicines and supporting information, and (5) try backup contacts if needed**. A non-emergency helper can call the nominated person first, as appropriate, but a serious emergency must never wait on a family call. The nominated first person in the editable contacts array is the primary person; the others are alternatives. Show relationship and readable phone number with a direct `tel:` action when one is available and consented.

## Evidence-led field hierarchy

### Tier 1 — front screen, immediately useful

1. **Identification**: full name, optionally age or birth year (full DOB only if strictly needed for identity matching, with consent). A photo may help identification but increases privacy risk; optional, not essential.
2. **Severe allergies/adverse reactions**: substance, nature/severity of reaction, whether anaphylaxis is known. DO NOT assume 'none' when absent. Clarify allergy versus intolerance or adverse reaction in the intake workflow.
3. **Critical medical conditions**: relevant to an emergency: diabetes/insulin use, epilepsy, severe asthma, adrenal insufficiency, serious heart problems, bleeding disorders, relevant cognitive/communication needs. Include only accurately confirmed conditions and plain-language notes, avoiding diagnostic/treatment instructions generated by software.
4. **High-impact medication risks**: particularly anticoagulants ('blood thinners'), insulin, steroid replacement and other medicines identified by their treating clinician as critical. Full up-to-date meds list available below.
5. **Emergency contacts**: at least two, names, relationships, numbers, and preferred order. Phone call actions should be large and clear; provide fallback if unanswered.
6. **Emergency call**: in Australia triple zero (000) is the urgent ambulance number; 112 is an alternative on mobile. Do not present the card as a medical advice service.

### Tier 2 — important supporting clinical details

7. **Current medicines**: generic/brand name, strength/dose, when taken, indication (optional), last confirmation; include OTC medicines, vitamins and complementary therapies. For PRN items, note 'as needed' and indication if verified. Consider last dose time only when clinically relevant and accurately updated (a static QR card rapidly becomes stale).
8. **Conditions/medical history**: concise relevant diagnoses; major operations only if they alter emergency care (e.g. organ transplant, dialysis).
9. **Implanted devices/treatments**: pacemaker, defibrillator, implanted pump, shunt, dialysis access or other relevant device; any critical details confirmed by clinician.
10. **Communication & assistance**: preferred language, hearing or vision assistance, speech impairment, interpreter need, cognitive impairment, mobility aid or other important assistance. Avoid unnecessary disclosure if not needed for an emergency.
11. **Clinician contact**: regular GP/practice name/phone, specialist when essential, relevant pharmacist or support service.
12. **Date last checked & reviewed by**: ideally confirmation of meds/allergies by a GP or pharmacist, with consent, and recency indicator. 'Reviewed' on a card is user-generated metadata unless proven otherwise.

### Tier 3 — optional and mainly hospital/administrative

13. **Blood group**: ONLY if clinically confirmed, and label 'For information only — hospital must test'. Hospital blood grouping and crossmatching are mandatory parts of safe transfusion processes; an NFC card does NOT establish compatibility.
14. **Insurance provider**: the insurer's name may assist administration, but policy/member details are not needed for immediate resuscitation and shouldn't be exposed on public scans.
15. **Advance care plan / medical treatment decision maker**: whether formal documents exist, and where to locate the signed document or who holds it. Avoid putting unverified 'DNR' labels in an open web profile. Legal validity and decisions vary by state; treating professionals need to confirm the directive and authority.
16. **My Health Record context**: in Australia, clinicians can access registered patients' information under applicable rules; the NFC site is NOT linked to My Health Record. The card may tell clinicians a recent summary exists, without including secure identifiers/passwords.

### Fields to keep private, not visible from a public NFC scan

Exact address, complete DOB where avoidable, Medicare/card number, Individual Healthcare Identifier, health fund membership/policy number, ID documents, whole medical record, sensitive notes, copies of directives, passwords, and access PINs. Emergency contacts' numbers are also third-party personal data: obtain their agreement before publishing them.

## Senior-specific design principles

- Clear purpose within 3 seconds, with 'CALL 000' high-contrast and separated from other contacts.
- Prioritise short plain-language labels; 16px+ body and large heading, avoid dense 3-column layouts on phones.
- Explicit 'Not provided' for missing items; don't suggest a negative finding unless medically confirmed.
- Simple large call targets; avoid animations, complex menus, popups, app installs, login steps or QR scanners that require accounts.
- Keep critical allergies and risks near top, visible without deep navigation; avoid colour-only distinction.
- Print view and non-digital backup in case of dead phones/network outages.
- WCAG 2.2 AA is an appropriate accessibility target (contrast >=4.5:1 for most normal text; 200% text resize without lost function). Conduct formal testing rather than claim compliance based on design intent alone.

## Privacy / security and regulatory reality

**Public GitHub Pages is not an appropriate storage system for a complete identifiable medical record.** Anyone with access to the URL can see the details; URL obscurity and `noindex` do not stop a deliberate visitor. A publicly readable NFC tag does not authenticate callers. Public databases, source code, logs and archived pages all have potential exposure. Treat the public view as information the owner knowingly agrees to make publicly accessible.

Australian privacy legislation treats health information as sensitive; applicable entities have consent, data collection, data minimisation, security, lifecycle and incident obligations. Applicability depends on actual service design and operations, so get qualified legal/privacy assessment before a commercial release. Verify explicit consent of the profile owner or properly authorised representative; separately inform and seek consent from emergency contacts before their details are shared publicly. Document withdrawal, correction, lost card response, replacement token, deletion/retention, and incident handling.

Suggested full-service architecture:

1. Public NFC/QR entry: minimal emergency card, explicitly consented, with critical facts only; secure HTTPS origin, hard-to-guess per-card identifiers, easy revocation. Hard-to-guess URLs are not access control and are not a substitute for genuine authorisation.
2. Separately secured private record: authenticated authorised access, encryption at rest/in transit, least privilege, editing controls and audit logs, backups, access logging and deletion. Avoid false promises of 'emergency override' unless established with governance and appropriate safeguards.
3. Account and caregiver workflow: consent, correction, verified revisions, change notifications and accessible management.
4. Data model with time stamps/source verification. Clinicians confirm medications and allergies; no automated instructions for treatment.
5. Use an Australian-appropriate compliant hosting/vendor arrangement, consent and records-management policy; evaluate Australian Privacy Principles and relevant jurisdictional legislation.
6. Periodic user prompts to recheck data (e.g. after new meds, admission, changed doses, changed contacts). A date does not guarantee freshness.

## Launch stages and clinical safety

**Stage A:** user-tested visual prototype (included here), anonymous or fictional data only.
**Stage B:** prototype user intake + independent clinical content review (GP/pharmacist), opt-in public emergency profile and privacy policy.
**Stage C:** secure system design/legal review/security assessment, appropriate hosting, audit, monitoring, deletion/revocation and recovery mechanisms.
**Stage D:** limited test with older adults/carers and relevant professionals, acceptance and comprehension evaluation. No guarantee that paramedics will scan or use a tag.

Potential users include older adults living independently, people with multiple prescriptions, people who may have difficulty communicating, care providers and family members, and anyone with serious allergies or implants. This is an optional personal information aid, not a substitute for recognised clinical systems or a staffed response service.

## Open questions for product validation

Will responders actually scan? What can safely be public versus confidential? How will carers keep medicines current? Which provider verifies the information? Can users delete/disable a lost or stolen tag? Can a person opt out of internet publication entirely? How does the service respond if consent or a person's capacity changes? How will caregivers be trained to update the card? How will offline fallback work?

## Prototype limitations

- NOT secure, encrypted or authenticated; does not store real data in a database.
- NOT linked to My Health Record, hospitals, ambulance dispatch, insurers or any electronic medical record.
- Does not generate actual NFC tags or QR codes. For NFC use, write the eventual page URL to a tag; QR can encode the same URL after safe deployment.
- `preview-demo.html` uses fictional text; emergency contacts have no real numbers. Only `000` is an active phone shortcut by default.
- Public profiles should be independently reviewed for compliance and clinical accuracy before real deployment.

## Existing solutions and the gap this product could fill

- **iPhone Medical ID:** Apple Health can display medical conditions, allergies, medications and emergency contacts from the locked phone when the user enables it. This is an existing free solution for many iPhone owners; your tag is primarily valuable when their phone is missing, flat or inaccessible, or when a helper recognises a physical NFC/QR card faster. https://support.apple.com/en-au/105072
- **Android Personal Safety / Emergency information:** supported Android phones can show emergency medical details and contacts on the lock screen, with privacy exposure noted by Google. Some features depend on device/Android version. https://support.google.com/android/answer/9319337/get-help-in-an-emergency-using-your-android-phone?hl=en-GB
- **MedicAlert and established medical IDs:** medical identification jewellery/services already help people communicate medication risks and allergies, and may integrate a more extensive profile. The strongest niche for your product is customisation, readability, simplicity, affordable tag/card issuance, family-supported updating and a printed fallback, not a claim to invent medical ID. https://www.medicalert.org/medicalert-digital-health-profile/
- **My Health Record (Australia):** established clinician-accessible health information including medicines, allergies and care planning. This website concept does not replace or integrate with My Health Record, and clinicians may prefer authorised clinical records. https://www.digitalhealth.gov.au/initiatives-and-programs/my-health-record

## Suggested usability experiment before investment

Recruit a small diverse voluntary group of older adults and caregivers and, with permission, relevant emergency-care professionals. Use anonymous mock profiles only. Measure: time to identify an emergency call, time to find a severe allergy, success tapping the first contact, number of navigation errors, readability outdoors, and acceptance/understanding of the privacy warning. Test on old Androids/iPhones, with and without NFC, and on low-bandwidth and offline conditions. Accessibility and emergency suitability are unproven until tested.
